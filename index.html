ESI and MALDI explorer

The whole process. A high-voltage supply holds the metal capillary at +3 to 5 kV and the inlet at ground, so the electric field points from the tip toward the inlet and charges the liquid as it leaves the tip. The liquid forms a Taylor cone and a jet that breaks into a spray of charged droplets carrying analyte molecules. Warm drying gas evaporates the solvent as the droplets drift toward the inlet; they shrink and split until the analyte leaves as gas-phase ions that enter the mass spectrometer.

Taylor cone. The metal capillary is the positive electrode and the inlet is at ground, so the field points from the tip toward the inlet. Positive ions in the liquid move with the field to the liquid surface at the tip, while negative ions move the other way, back toward the positive capillary wall. The excess positive charge at the surface pulls the liquid into a cone, and a thin jet from its tip breaks into droplets that carry that charge along with dissolved analyte.

Evaporation and fission. Solvent evaporates but the charge stays, so the droplet shrinks until it reaches the Rayleigh limit, where charge repulsion matches surface tension. It then undergoes fission and sheds small, highly charged droplets. The remaining droplet keeps shrinking until the electric field at its surface is strong enough to lift the analyte out as an ion (field-assisted ion desorption).

Ion release. Two models explain the last step. Small ions are pushed out of a tiny, highly charged droplet by the field at its surface (ion evaporation, Iribarne and Thomson). Larger molecules such as peptides and proteins stay until the last solvent is gone, and the remaining protons stay on their basic sites (charged residue, Dole).

Why so many charge states? When a droplet dries out, its molecule keeps the charges the droplet still holds, up to the number of basic sites that can take a proton; this peptide has four (the N-terminal amine and three side-chain amines). Droplets differ in size and charge, so identical molecules end up with different numbers of protons: [M + 2H]2+, [M + 3H]3+ and [M + 4H]4+. Each charge state gives its own peak. A protein with dozens of basic sites spreads over many more, such as +11 to +17 for cytochrome c.

Into the analyzer. The same protein arrives with different numbers of protons, so one molecule gives a series of peaks. Each peak appears at m/z = (M + n·mH)/n, so more protons means lower m/z. Two neighboring peaks are enough to find n and M.

The whole process. A short UV laser pulse hits a spot of matrix crystals that contain the analyte. The matrix absorbs the light and erupts into a plume, where protons pass from matrix to analyte. The target plate is held at +20 kV and the grid at ground, so positive ions are pushed into the time-of-flight tube, where lighter ions reach the detector first.

Sample spot. The analyte is mixed with a large excess of a UV-absorbing matrix, here 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB), typically about 1,000 to 10,000 matrix molecules per analyte molecule. As the droplet dries on the metal target, the matrix crystallizes and traps the analyte molecules, keeping them apart from one another.

Laser pulse. A pulse of UV light lasting a few nanoseconds is absorbed by the matrix, not the analyte. Matrix molecules near the surface are excited (M + hν → M*), and the absorbed energy heats the top of the crystal almost instantly.

Ablation. The sudden heating blows the top layers off the crystal as a dense, expanding plume of matrix molecules and small clusters that still hold analyte molecules. Because the matrix takes up most of the energy, the analyte goes into the gas phase without breaking apart.

Proton transfer. In the hot, dense plume, an excited matrix molecule gives a proton to another matrix molecule: M* + M → MH+ + (M−H)−. The protonated matrix then hands its proton to the analyte, which holds a proton more strongly: MH+ + A → AH+ + M. Here M is the matrix and A the analyte.
